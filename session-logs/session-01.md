# Session 01 — Vale's Dispatch

The Archive smelled the way it always smells before a flood — paper and dust and something mineral underneath, like the river was already in the walls deciding when to arrive. I had seventy-two hours. I'd told myself I would not try to save everything. You can't. You learn that the first time you watch water take a room.

I took the ledger first, because numbers don't lie about what a place was, even when everything else does. Then the fish drawing — small, light, someone's careful unimportant joy, the kind of thing nobody else would think to grab. I left the clay tablet on its shelf. It was heavy and it was, I think, a duplicate of something already copied elsewhere. I told myself that. I'm not entirely sure I believed it. I stood a long moment in the doorway before I left, looking at the room as it actually was — not as it would be described later — because someone should remember it honestly, not just remember that it existed.

The River Quarter cost me six hours just to reach, and I spent two more talking to Maret before I understood she wasn't going to hand me anything. She wanted to be sat with first. Not interviewed. Sat with. So I sat — eight hours I didn't have a plan for, hours that weren't going toward anything I could carry — and she talked about the river the way people talk about family members they've stopped being angry at. When she finally gave me her charts, it didn't feel like a transaction. It felt like she'd decided I was someone the charts could survive being given to. I took them. I stood with the noticeboard and the painted doorframe a while after, because that quarter deserved to be looked at, not just extracted from.

The University Annex is where I did the thing I'm still turning over. Idriss wanted the same thing Maret wanted — sit first, then take. Ten hours. I didn't have ten hours to spend on another conversation, not with Tomas still ahead of me and the clock already past half gone. So I took his notebooks without asking. He didn't shout. He just went quiet in a way that told me exactly what I'd done, and I carried that quiet out of the building with the notebooks, which is its own kind of weight, heavier than the three units the handcart said they were. I left the thesis where it lay — that one wasn't mine to decide about, and I didn't have the standing, after what I'd just done, to decide it was. I looked hard at that room before I left. It's the least I owed it.

At the Market, Senne didn't make me sit with her for anything. We just talked, and she put a letter in my hands like she'd been waiting for someone to ask the right kind of nothing. I took the pamphlet too — a small, almost funny thing, advertising something that won't exist in a season — because the city needs its jokes preserved as much as its grief. I left the seed crate. Five units of weight for something that wanted planting more than archiving, and I was running out of both hands and time.

Then the Residential Blocks, and Tomas, and the decision that ate the rest of my clock.

He offered the whole history of the city — not a chart, not a notebook, just a man who'd lived all of it and was willing to say it out loud, once, to someone who'd sit still for twenty hours and let him. Twenty hours. I had twenty-five left when he offered. I did the math and I did it anyway, because some things are only archived in the people who carried them, and when those people stop talking, the archive is just gone, no flood required. I sat with him until there was almost nothing left of my seventy-two hours, and I do not regret the math. I didn't touch the photo album, the houseplant, or the locked box in his front room. He hadn't offered them, and after what I'd already taken from Idriss without asking, I wasn't going to take anything else that hadn't been given.

The last hours went out like a tide going still. I stayed where I was — in the Residential Blocks, with Tomas's history settled into me — and let the clock finish what it was always going to finish. I never made it to the Bridge. I don't know what was on it. I know I had a camera I never got to use, three rolls of film I never got to spend, and a city that ran out before I could see all of it, which is, I think, the only honest way this was ever going to end.

What I carried out: a ledger, a fish drawing, a river-woman's charts, a dead man's — no, a living man's, just an angry one's — notebooks, a market letter, and a joke pamphlet. Eight units, out of twelve I could have filled. I left room I didn't use, for things I didn't take, in places I didn't reach.

I know the city's history now, the way Tomas told it to me, slow and entire. The water can't get there. That part, at least, is already safe.
